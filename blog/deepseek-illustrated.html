DeepSeek V3 → V3.2, illustrated

A picture-first walk through Sebastian Raschka's technical review of DeepSeek's model line — what changed in the architecture, what changed in the training, and why it matters.

What this is. This is not a DeepSeek paper itself. It is Sebastian Raschka's technical review of DeepSeek's model line from V3 to V3.2, plus the side releases around it. The article answers three questions: (1) what changed architecturally? (2) what changed in reasoning / RL training? (3) why do those changes matter?

The article's core claim is simple:

  • DeepSeek V3 established the base architecture.
  • R1 showed how far strong reasoning post-training could go on top of that base.
  • V3.1, V3.2-Exp, DeepSeekMath V2, and V3.2 show DeepSeek kept improving on two fronts at once: inference efficiency / attention design, and reasoning / RL / self-verification training.

So the PDF is really about evolution, not one isolated model.

The big picture

The article is built around three layers.

1. Hybrid reasoning vs dedicated reasoning

One of the first things Raschka emphasizes is that DeepSeek changed product shape, not just internals.

2. MLA — the existing efficiency base

Before V3.2, DeepSeek already had an efficiency idea: Multi-Head Latent Attention (MLA). Instead of storing huge full key/value states for every token, DeepSeek compresses them into a latent form for the KV cache, then reconstructs what's needed later.

3. The main architecture change — DSA

This is the most important architecture section in the article. V3.2-Exp and V3.2 use DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA). The baseline comparison: normal attention lets a token look at all earlier tokens; sliding-window lets it look at a recent fixed window; DSA lets it look at a selected subset — and the subset is not fixed, it is learned.

How DSA works

Raschka describes two pieces: a lightning indexer that scores how relevant each past token is to the current query (a scaled dot product passed through ReLU, with per-head learned weights, computed over MLA's compressed representations), and a token selector that keeps only the top-scoring tokens (top-k, set to 2048 in the released code). The current token then attends to just those.

The honest bar. DSA's goal was not to beat V3.1-Terminus. It was to keep the quality drop from sparsity small while getting big efficiency gains. The real success criterion is the quality-per-cost tradeoff — which is also why V3.2-Exp shipped first: to get the serving infrastructure ready for this non-standard attention.

DSA in depth: a cheap scout, then a careful read

The key structure is that DSA splits attention into two steps: a cheap scouting step that scores many past tokens, and a more expensive real-attention step that runs over only the few chosen ones.

Standard vs sliding window vs retrieval vs DSA

A sliding window assumes relevance is mostly local — recent tokens matter most. Useful, but crude. DSA drops that assumption: the most useful past token might be far back, so let the model learn to find it, wherever it sits.

The catch: this only works if the selector is good. If it misses an important old token, quality drops. So the whole design challenge is whether the model can learn to choose the right sparse subset — which is why Raschka frames DSA as an efficiency move whose success criterion is "less quality lost per unit of speed and cost saved."

4. DeepSeekMath V2 — the important side story

Raschka spends real time here, and it's not filler: it's a proof-of-concept for techniques that land in V3.2. Its setup has three roles — a proof generator, a proof verifier, and a meta-verifier.

5. Why separate verifier training matters

Classic self-refinement uses one model to generate, critique, and revise. The DeepSeek team found a weakness: a model prompted to generate and check its own proof in one shot "tends to claim correctness even when the external verifier easily identifies flaws." That is bluffing.

The subtle, high-signal claim: the separate verifier is essential during training, but at inference you can collapse back to one model — because that model was already shaped by a stronger verifier and meta-verifier. It isn't naive self-critique; it's self-critique after being trained under an external critic.

Plain version. "Just have the model check its own work" is not the lesson. The lesson is: self-checking becomes trustworthy only after the model has been trained with a better checking process than it would invent on its own. Same student, but grown up under a strict grader — now it can grade itself.

Self-refinement, and the inference-cost tradeoff

The training loop, turn by turn

Put the three roles in motion. One training turn: the generator writes a proof; the verifier scores it against the rubric; the meta-verifier checks that the verifier judged fairly; the combined signal updates the models. Repeat.

Expensive where it matters, cheap where you scale

The education analogy. Naive self-refinement is a student who writes an essay, grades it, and revises it — having never been taught by a strong teacher. DeepSeek's setup is a student who writes, a teacher who critiques, and a senior examiner who checks the teacher; the student revises under that supervision. After enough of this, the student can self-edit far better alone. At inference you get the graduated student, not the naive one — self-critique is a reenacted learned behavior, not something invented from scratch.
Why it's powerful, in two lines. (1) Better training signal — the generator is rewarded in a structured environment (proof quality, verification correctness, revision quality), not for lucky answers. (2) Cheaper inference — once the verifier style is internalized, one model replaces the generator + verifier + meta-verifier stack.

6. The RL story — from R1 to V3.2

The continuity: still in the RLVR family, still using GRPO-style ideas. The change: reward design got wider and more task-specific. R1 used a format reward, a language-consistency reward, and a main verifier reward for math/code. V3.2 changed this.

GRPO updates — conservative on purpose

The article compares DeepSeek's choices to newer variants like DAPO and Dr. GRPO, and the framing is that DeepSeek is relatively conservative: it keeps a KL term (tuned per domain, near zero for math rather than removed), keeps the original normalization, and adds targeted fixes — unbiased KL estimate, off-policy sequence masking, keeping the MoE routing and sampling mask from the rollout. The point isn't a GRPO revolution; it's that DeepSeek cares a lot about stable training behavior over novelty.

7 & 8. V3.2 proper, and V3.2-Speciale

When Raschka reaches V3.2, the message is: this is where the puzzle pieces come together — the strong base architecture, MLA, DSA, hybrid reasoning, stronger RL training, and the verifier / self-refinement lessons. And it's not just a math model: it's aimed at tools, code, and agentic tasks too. See it as a flagship general-purpose open-weight model with serious reasoning, not only a reasoner and not only an efficiency play.

V3.2-Speciale is the extended-thinking variant — trained on reasoning data with a reduced length penalty so it writes longer. Same old tradeoff: longer reasoning tends to raise accuracy, at more tokens. Strong reasoning comes not only from bigger models but from inference-time compute choices and structured refinement.

9. The appendix-like mHC mention

At the end there's a mention of mHC (manifold-constrained hyper-connections). This is side material, not the spine. The rough theme: widen the residual "pipe" into several interacting streams, with a norm-preserving / stability-oriented constraint on how they mix.

What I think the most important ideas are

  1. DSA is the main architecture story — learned sparse attention on top of MLA; efficiency is the reason; the success criterion is quality preserved per unit cost.
  2. Verifier training is one of the most interesting training ideas — generator + verifier + meta-verifier; external verification strength used during training; later collapse to a single model at inference.
  3. DeepSeek is shifting from separate-reasoning-model logic toward hybrid flagship logic — more product-integrated, more generally usable.
  4. RL reward design is becoming mixed — symbolic where possible, generative where necessary.
  5. The real theme is not "one clever trick" — it is iterative system-building: each release adds one more layer of efficiency, reasoning control, or deployment readiness.

What the PDF is not saying

  • It is not claiming DSA is a universal replacement for all attention.
  • It is not claiming self-verification works automatically.
  • It is not claiming V3.2 is only a reasoning model.
  • It is not mainly a benchmark chest-thumping article.
  • It is not centered on one radical, theorem-level idea.

It is much more: here is how a high-end open-weight line evolved through a sequence of architecture and training decisions.

My honest evaluation

The strongest idea in the whole piece is not the sparse attention alone. The most important practical insight is probably this:

A separate verifier may be essential during training even if you later do not need a separate verifier at inference. That's a deep systems insight: don't judge self-refinement by testing naive same-model critique — a model can become much better at self-critique after being trained under stronger external checking.

The second strongest idea: DSA is a good example of learned sparsity used for inference efficiency, not architecture novelty for its own sake. A mature engineering direction.

The PDF in one paragraph

DeepSeek's path from V3 to V3.2 is the story of a base open-weight architecture being progressively upgraded in two ways: first, by making attention and inference more efficient through MLA plus learned sparse attention (DSA); and second, by making reasoning more reliable through RLVR, hybrid reasoning training, self-verification, and verifier-guided refinement. The article's deepest message is that strong modern LLMs are no longer improving mainly through one isolated trick — they improve through layered coordination between architecture, reward design, verification, and inference-time compute.
